Study Guide

1. Driving Safely

Vehicle inspection habits

  • Inspect your vehicle before every trip — federal and state law require it.
  • Check brakes, tires, lights/reflectors, mirrors, coupling, steering, horn, wipers, emergency equipment.
  • Carry spare fuses (unless vehicle has circuit breakers), three reflective triangles, and a properly charged fire extinguisher.

Basic control & shifting

  • With a manual transmission, the two main methods of shifting up are double-clutching and pre-selecting the next gear by engine rpm or road speed.
  • Never coast with the clutch pedal pressed or in neutral — it's illegal and dangerous.
  • Back up only when necessary; back toward the driver's side when you can so you can see better.
  • Use the "GOAL" rule: Get Out And Look before backing.

Seeing & communicating

  • Look 12–15 seconds ahead (about a quarter-mile at highway speed).
  • Check mirrors regularly; scan, don't stare.
  • Signal early, signal continuously, and cancel the signal after the maneuver.
  • Use four-way flashers if stopped or moving slowly. At night or in low visibility, tap the brake pedal to warn traffic behind.

Space management

  • Following distance: at speeds up to 40 mph, allow 1 second for every 10 feet of vehicle length. Over 40 mph, add 1 second.
  • Example: a 60-ft combination at 55 mph needs at least 7 seconds following distance.
  • Keep space overhead (at least 14 feet clearance is typical, but check each route).

Speed management

  • Stopping distance = perception distance + reaction distance + braking distance.
  • Total stopping distance at 55 mph on dry pavement is roughly ~419 ft (about the length of a football field).
  • Reduce speed by about 1/3 on wet roads; by 1/2 or more on packed snow; slow to a crawl on ice.
  • Slow before a curve; accelerate gently through it.

Night & bad-weather driving

  • Use low beams when within 500 ft of an oncoming vehicle or following within 500 ft.
  • In fog, use low beams (high beams reflect back).
  • If your drive wheels skid: take your foot off the accelerator, push in the clutch, steer in the direction you want to go, then counter-steer to avoid over-correcting.

Mountain driving

  • Select a safe speed, a low gear, and use the proper braking technique before starting down.
  • Use light, steady pressure — don't "fan" (pump) the brakes, which overheats them.
  • Brake drum overheating causes brake fade. Always test brakes at the top of the grade.
  • Use escape ramps if brakes fail — don't hesitate.

Hazards, accidents, and fires

  • Place reflective triangles within 10 minutes of stopping: 10 ft behind, 100 ft behind, 100 ft ahead (on two-lane roads). On divided/one-way roads put all three behind the vehicle.
  • For tire fires, cool them — a fire extinguisher alone won't put them out.
  • Know your extinguisher: A=ordinary combustibles, B=flammable liquids, C=electrical, D=metals. B:C or A:B:C extinguishers are required in commercial vehicles.

Fatigue, alcohol, drugs

  • CDL BAC limit is 0.04% — half the limit for non-CDL drivers.
  • Refusing a test has the same consequences as failing it.
  • Coffee and fresh air do not sober you up or cure fatigue — only rest does.
Key numbers to memorize
  • BAC limit for CDL: 0.04%
  • Stopping distance at 55 mph on dry pavement: ~419 ft
  • Look ahead: 12–15 seconds
  • Triangles: 10 ft, 100 ft, 100 ft
  • Passing score on knowledge test: 80%
2. Transporting Cargo Safely

Driver responsibilities

  • The driver is responsible for inspecting the cargo, recognizing overloads and poorly balanced weight, making sure it is properly secured, and checking it periodically.
  • Inspect cargo within the first 50 miles, then every 150 miles or 3 hours (whichever comes first), and any time you change duty status.

Weight & balance

  • GVWR = Gross Vehicle Weight Rating (max total weight of the vehicle).
  • GAWR = Gross Axle Weight Rating (max weight per axle).
  • GCWR = Gross Combination Weight Rating (truck + trailer + load).
  • Put heavy cargo low and centered to keep the center of gravity low — reduces rollover risk.
  • Poor weight distribution causes steering problems, unstable handling, and shortens brake life.

Securing cargo

  • Use tiedowns rated for the cargo weight. Minimum of 1 tiedown for every 10 ft of cargo, at least 2 total.
  • Header boards ("headache racks") protect the driver from shifting cargo.
  • Blocking and bracing prevent cargo from sliding.

Special cargo

  • Sealed loads: you can't inspect them, but you are still responsible for not overloading.
  • Oversize loads: may need permits, escorts, and special route / time restrictions.
  • Hazardous materials: requires hazmat endorsement and proper placarding.
  • Livestock: shifts weight; take curves and turns slowly — use a false bulkhead for partial loads.
  • Liquid tankers: liquid surge affects handling; bulkheads/baffles help but smooth bore tanks are extra sensitive.
Key numbers to memorize
  • Cargo re-inspection: within 50 miles, then every 150 mi / 3 hrs.
  • Tiedowns: minimum 1 per 10 ft of cargo, at least 2 total.
3. Air Brakes

Parts of the system

  • Compressor pumps air into the storage tanks.
  • Governor controls when the compressor pumps air (cut-in around 100 psi, cut-out around 125 psi).
  • Air storage tanks hold compressed air. Drain daily — water and oil collect there.
  • Foot valve (brake pedal) releases air from the tanks to apply the brakes.
  • Brake chambers, slack adjusters, S-cam brakes convert air pressure into mechanical braking force.
  • Supply pressure gauge, application pressure gauge, and the low air warning (activates before pressure drops below 60 psi).

Dual air brake systems

  • Most heavy trucks have two separate air systems (one for each axle group). If one fails, the other still works.
  • Before driving, let both systems build up to at least 100 psi.

Spring / parking brakes

  • Spring brakes come on automatically when air pressure drops into the 20–45 psi range.
  • Never use the parking brake when the brakes are very hot (can damage them) or frozen wet.

Air brake inspection (the static / "leak" checks)

  • Chock wheels if needed; release parking brakes.
  • Air leak test (static): with governor cut-out, engine off, release service brake, wait 1 min — pressure drop must be no more than 2 psi in 1 minute (single vehicle) or 3 psi in 1 minute (combination).
  • Air leak test (applied): apply full brake pedal for 1 minute — drop must be no more than 3 psi (single) or 4 psi (combination).
  • Low-air warning: fan the brake; warning must come on at or before 60 psi.
  • Spring brake activation: continue fanning; parking/spring brakes must come on at 20–45 psi.
  • Buildup time: pressure must build from 85 to 100 psi in 45 seconds or less in dual systems.

Using air brakes

  • Normal stop: press the pedal smoothly; release as you come to a stop.
  • Controlled (threshold) braking: brake just hard enough to keep wheels from locking; maintain steering.
  • Stab braking (non-ABS): fully apply brakes until wheels lock, release briefly, re-apply — only for emergencies.
  • Air brakes have brake lag — an added ~½ second delay before they actually apply. Build that into your following distance.
Key numbers to memorize
  • Low-air warning activates at/before 60 psi.
  • Spring brakes auto-apply at 20–45 psi.
  • Governor cut-out: about 125 psi; cut-in: about 100 psi.
  • Static leak: ≤ 2 psi/min (single), ≤ 3 psi/min (combo).
  • Applied leak: ≤ 3 psi/min (single), ≤ 4 psi/min (combo).
  • Buildup 85→100 psi in ≤ 45 seconds.
4. Combination Vehicles

Handling a combination safely

  • Combinations are heavier, longer, and have more blind spots than straight trucks.
  • Off-tracking: trailer wheels follow a tighter path than the tractor through turns — make wide right turns, don't cut the corner.
  • Rearward amplification ("crack-the-whip"): a sudden steer at the tractor is amplified at the trailer. Doubles and triples have the most. Steer gently.
  • A high center of gravity makes combinations prone to roll over. Take curves below the posted speed, especially when loaded high.

Antilock brakes (ABS)

  • ABS keeps wheels from locking during hard braking so you can keep steering.
  • ABS does not shorten your stopping distance — brake normally, let the ABS do its job.
  • A yellow malfunction lamp on the dash (or trailer, post-2001) means ABS is not working — brakes still work, but without the anti-lock benefit.

Coupling a tractor to a trailer

  1. Inspect fifth wheel: no damaged/missing parts, plate greased, jaws open, safety latch/lock released.
  2. Inspect trailer: kingpin and apron undamaged; trailer at correct height (just low enough that tractor raises it slightly when backing under).
  3. Line up tractor; back slowly straight under the trailer until the kingpin engages.
  4. Pull forward gently against the kingpin with the trailer brakes locked to test the connection.
  5. Visually check the fifth-wheel lock from underneath — jaws must be closed around the shank of the kingpin.
  6. Connect the electrical cord and air lines (red = emergency, blue = service). Check for leaks.
  7. Raise the landing gear fully; secure the crank handle.

Uncoupling

  1. Position the rig straight.
  2. Ease pressure on the fifth-wheel locking jaws (back slightly against trailer brakes with tractor parking brake, then set the trailer brakes, then release tractor parking brake… sequence from the manual).
  3. Lower the landing gear just until it touches the ground; add turns only if trailer is loaded heavily.
  4. Disconnect air lines and electrical; secure them.
  5. Release the fifth-wheel latch; pull tractor partially clear; secure the tractor; finish lowering landing gear; pull the tractor clear.
Key numbers to memorize
  • Air lines: red = emergency, blue = service.
  • Rearward amplification increases with each added trailer.
  • ABS does NOT reduce stopping distance.
5. Vehicle Inspection

Why inspect?

  • Safety is the primary reason — defects cause crashes and breakdowns.
  • Federal and state law require pre-trip, en-route, and post-trip inspections.
  • DOT inspectors can put your truck out of service for serious defects.

Types of inspection

  • Pre-trip: before every trip, systematic walk-around.
  • En-route: check gauges regularly; check tires, brakes, cargo, lights each stop.
  • Post-trip: complete a Driver Vehicle Inspection Report (DVIR) listing any defects.

The 7-step inspection method

  1. Vehicle overview — review last DVIR, look for leaks/damage under the truck.
  2. Check engine compartment — fluid levels (oil, coolant, power steering, windshield washer), belts, hoses, wires.
  3. Start the engine and inspect inside the cab — gauges (oil pressure, air pressure, ammeter/voltmeter, coolant temp), mirrors, windshield, steering play, horn, wipers/washers, heater/defroster, emergency equipment (3 triangles, fuses, extinguisher).
  4. Turn off engine and check lights — headlights (high/low), parking, clearance, taillights, brake lights, turn signals, 4-way flashers.
  5. Do the walk-around — tires (tread depth: 4/32 inch on steering axle, 2/32 inch on other axles), wheels/rims, suspension, brakes (drums, hoses, slack adjusters), coupling (fifth wheel, kingpin, air/electrical), lights, reflectors.
  6. Check signal lights — have someone help or use walk-around check.
  7. Start engine & brake system check — test service brake (pedal firm, no pull), parking brake, hydraulic or air brake buildup/leak tests.

Out-of-service triggers (examples)

  • Steering axle tire below 4/32" tread; other axle below 2/32".
  • Flat tire, audible air leak, exposed cord or ply.
  • Cracked wheels, loose or missing lug nuts.
  • Any broken or loose part of the steering, suspension, or brake system.
Key numbers to memorize
  • Tire tread: 4/32" steer axle, 2/32" other axles.
  • Emergency kit: 3 reflective triangles, spare fuses, charged fire extinguisher.
  • Required fire extinguisher: B:C (or A:B:C).
6. Basic Vehicle Control & Skills Test

What the skills test covers

  • Pre-trip inspection (demonstrated and explained out loud to the examiner).
  • Basic vehicle control — backing maneuvers on a range.
  • On-road driving test — turns, intersections, lane use, railway crossings, bridges, and stops.

Common basic-control maneuvers

  • Straight-line backing — back in a straight line between two rows of cones without crossing them.
  • Offset backing (left/right) — back from one lane into a parallel lane offset to the side.
  • Parallel park (driver side and/or conventional).
  • Alley dock — back at a 90-degree angle into a simulated loading dock.
  • Each maneuver is scored on pull-ups (times you pull forward to correct), encroachments (crossing a boundary line), and final position.

Turning & mirrors

  • Swing wide enough on right turns so the trailer wheels clear the curb. Don't swing into the oncoming lane before the turn — that invites someone to pass on your right.
  • Check mirrors every 5–8 seconds. Adjust them before starting the trip.
  • Use GOAL any time you're unsure about clearance or hazards behind you.

On-road test tips

  • Wear your seat belt, signal every lane change, come to complete stops.
  • At uncontrolled railway crossings with a bus or hazmat load, stop 15–50 ft before the tracks.
  • Narrate what you're doing when in doubt — examiners want to see that you're thinking about safety.
Key numbers to memorize
  • Railroad crossing stop (hazmat/bus): 15–50 feet before tracks.
  • Mirror scan interval: about every 5–8 seconds.
  • GOAL = Get Out And Look.
